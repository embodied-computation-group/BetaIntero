# Results

## Interoceptive Perception: Heartbeat Discrimination Task

To examine whether beta-adrenergic blockade altered interoceptive perceptual accuracy, we fit a binomial generalised linear mixed model (GLMM) predicting trial-level heartbeat discrimination responses (1 = "faster," 0 = "slower") from Drug condition (placebo [reference], bisoprolol, propranolol), listen BPM (subject-mean-centred), response BPM (subject-mean-centred), trial number, and visit, with the critical test being the Drug × Listen BPM interaction. Random effects included per-subject intercepts and slopes for listen BPM and response BPM. One participant (sub_4071) was excluded due to an implausible individual slope indicative of near-perfect separation. The model was fit via maximum likelihood using the `glmmTMB` package in R (51 participants; 8,680 trials; AIC = 6,826.41).

Response BPM was a strong positive predictor of reporting "faster" (*B* = 0.26, *SE* = 0.02, *z* = 15.06, *p* < .001), confirming that participants were sensitive to the auditory stimulus rate. Listen BPM — reflecting the participant's actual heart rate on each trial — was a significant negative predictor under placebo (*B* = −0.08, *SE* = 0.02, *z* = −4.11, *p* < .001), indicating that higher heart rates were associated with a reduced probability of reporting the tones as "faster," consistent with an interoceptive anchoring effect. Trial number was a significant positive predictor (*B* = 0.01, *SE* = 0.002, *z* = 7.10, *p* < .001), suggesting a slight shift toward "faster" responses over the course of the session.

Critically, the Drug × Listen BPM interaction was significant for both beta-blockers. Relative to placebo, the negative listen BPM slope was attenuated under propranolol (*B* = 0.06, *SE* = 0.01, *z* = 3.92, *p* < .001) and bisoprolol (*B* = 0.03, *SE* = 0.01, *z* = 2.12, *p* = .034). Post-hoc simple slopes (estimated via `emtrends`) confirmed that the listen BPM slope was significantly negative under placebo (*B* = −0.080, *SE* = 0.019, 95% CI [−0.118, −0.042]) and bisoprolol (*B* = −0.049, *SE* = 0.020, 95% CI [−0.089, −0.010]), but was not significantly different from zero under propranolol (*B* = −0.022, *SE* = 0.020, 95% CI [−0.061, 0.018]). Pairwise comparison of slopes confirmed a significant difference between placebo and propranolol (*z* = −3.92, *p* < .001), with the placebo–bisoprolol contrast approaching significance (*z* = −2.12, *p* = .086) and no significant difference between bisoprolol and propranolol (*z* = −1.77, *p* = .179). These results indicate that beta-adrenergic blockade — particularly propranolol — attenuated the relationship between cardiac activity and heartbeat discrimination, consistent with a reduction in the interoceptive signal available to guide perceptual judgements (Figure 1).

## Metacognitive Confidence

To examine whether beta-adrenergic blockade affected metacognitive confidence, we fit ordered beta regression models to trial-level confidence ratings (scaled 0–1) separately for the respiratory (RRST) and cardiac (HRD) tasks. Ordered beta regression accommodates the bounded nature of confidence ratings, including boundary responses at 0 and 1, via a three-component mixture (a beta distribution for continuous interior values plus point masses at each boundary). All models were fit with `glmmTMB` using the `ordbeta()` family.

### Respiratory Interoception (RRST)

The RRST model predicted confidence from Drug (placebo [reference], propranolol, bisoprolol), Accuracy (correct [reference], incorrect), their interaction, and subject-mean-centred stimulus intensity, with per-subject random intercepts and random slopes for stimulus intensity and accuracy (48 participants; 8,628 trials; AIC = 9,167.25).

Stimulus intensity was a strong positive predictor of confidence (*B* = 0.28, *SE* = 0.02, *z* = 14.02, *p* < .001), indicating that participants were more confident on trials with higher stimulus intensities. Accuracy was also significant: incorrect responses were associated with substantially lower confidence (*B* = −0.56, *SE* = 0.06, *z* = −9.65, *p* < .001), confirming intact metacognitive sensitivity. Crucially, neither Drug main effect nor the Drug × Accuracy interaction reached significance (all *p*s > .320), indicating that beta-adrenergic blockade did not alter respiratory metacognitive confidence. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons of Drug within correct trials confirmed no differences (all *p*s > .583, Tukey-adjusted).

### Cardiac Interoception (HRD)

The HRD model predicted confidence from Drug, Response Correctness (correct [reference], incorrect), their interaction, and within-subject z-scored listen BPM, with per-subject random intercepts and random slopes for correctness and BPM (49 participants; 8,299 trials; one participant [sub_4049] excluded; AIC = 5,126.10).

Within-subject BPM was a significant positive predictor of confidence (*B* = 0.15, *SE* = 0.02, *z* = 6.21, *p* < .001), indicating that trials with higher heart rates elicited greater confidence. Incorrect responses were associated with lower confidence (*B* = −0.38, *SE* = 0.07, *z* = −5.21, *p* < .001), again confirming metacognitive sensitivity.

Both Drug main effects were significant: relative to placebo, confidence was higher under propranolol (*B* = 0.14, *SE* = 0.04, *z* = 3.61, *p* < .001) and bisoprolol (*B* = 0.24, *SE* = 0.04, *z* = 6.16, *p* < .001). The Drug × Correctness interaction was also significant for both bisoprolol (*B* = −0.16, *SE* = 0.06, *z* = −2.61, *p* = .009) and propranolol (*B* = −0.12, *SE* = 0.06, *z* = −2.01, *p* = .045), indicating that the drug-related increase in confidence was selective to correct trials.

Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Holm-adjusted) confirmed this pattern. On correct trials, confidence was significantly higher under propranolol than placebo (*z* = −3.61, *p* < .001), higher under bisoprolol than placebo (*z* = −6.16, *p* < .001), and higher under bisoprolol than propranolol (*z* = −3.18, *p* = .001). On incorrect trials, no pairwise Drug differences reached significance (all *p*s > .534). This dissociation indicates that beta-blockade selectively increased confidence when participants were correct, consistent with an enhancement of cardiac metacognitive sensitivity rather than a general confidence bias (Figure 2).
